4.1 Kinematic Modelling Overview

This page consolidates the forward and inverse kinematic modelling workflow in expandable sections. The first tab presents the geometric forward map from joint-space inputs to Cartesian output location, while the second tab documents the constrained inverse reconstruction used to recover joint angles from a target end-point.

4.1.1 Forward Kinematics

This section presents the MatLab Version of Forward Kinematics which will later be integrated into the firmware. The implementation is based on the geometric construction outlined in the function FK_G_from_phi1phi2 in VMC_WN_FK_Ainimation.m. The mechanism is modeled as a planar linkage with inputs $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$, and output point $\mathbf{G}$.

Forward kinematics was first derived by hand and later transferred to MATLAB for verification. The variables $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are the two motor-driven joint angles after chain and gearbox ratio conversion. In this report, symbols of the form $\phi$ denote absolute angles referenced to the global horizontal axis, while symbols of the form $\theta$ denote relative inter-link or inter-joint angles.

4.1.1.1 Angle Wrapping and Inputs

To ensure angular consistency, all angles are wrapped to $(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]$:

\[ \mathcal{W}(\alpha) = \operatorname{mod}(\alpha + 180,\, 360) - 180. \] \[ \phi_1 \leftarrow \mathcal{W}(\phi_1), \qquad \phi_2 \leftarrow \mathcal{W}(\phi_2). \]

Trigonometric operations are evaluated in degrees, consistent with MATLAB sind, cosd, and asind.

4.1.1.2 Intermediate Geometric Angles

\[ a_1 = \mathcal{W}\!\left(\frac{\phi_1 + \phi_2}{2}\right), \qquad a_3 = \mathcal{W}\!\left(\frac{180 - (\phi_1 + \phi_2)}{2}\right). \] \[ x = \operatorname{sat}_{[-1,1]}\!\left(\frac{L_2}{L_3}\sin(a_3)\right), \qquad a_2 = \mathcal{W}\!\left(\sin^{-1}(x)\right). \] \[ \phi_4 = \mathcal{W}(a_3 + \phi_2 - a_2), \qquad \phi_3 = \mathcal{W}(180 - a_2 - a_3 - \phi_2). \] \[ a_4 = \mathcal{W}(180 - \phi_1 - \phi_4), \qquad a_5 = \mathcal{W}(180 - a_4 + k_{a5}), \qquad a_6 = \mathcal{W}(a_5 - \phi_1). \]

4.1.1.3 Point-Wise Forward Kinematic Chain

\[ \mathbf{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{O} + \begin{bmatrix} L_2\cos\phi_2\\ -L_2\sin\phi_2 \end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A} + \begin{bmatrix} -L_3\cos\phi_3\\ -L_3\sin\phi_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{B} + \begin{bmatrix} -L_4\cos\phi_4\\ \;\;L_4\sin\phi_4 \end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{C} + \begin{bmatrix} -L_5\cos a_6\\ \;\;L_5\sin a_6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D} + \begin{bmatrix} -L_6\cos\phi_1\\ -L_6\sin\phi_1 \end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{O} + \begin{bmatrix} -L_1\cos\phi_1\\ -L_1\sin\phi_1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

4.1.1.4 Output Orientation and End-Point

\[ \phi_5 = \mathcal{W}\!\Big( k_{163} -\big(180-(\phi_1+a_6)\big) -\phi_1 -a_7 \Big), \] \[ \mathbf{G} = \mathbf{F} + \begin{bmatrix} L_7\cos\phi_5\\ -L_7\sin\phi_5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

So, the forward kinematic map is \[ (\phi_1,\phi_2)\mapsto \mathbf{G}(\phi_1,\phi_2), \] with intermediate points $\mathbf{O},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{F}$ recovered simultaneously.

4.1.2 Inverse Kinematics

This section presents the inverse-kinematic method used to recover the input angles $(\phi_1,\phi_2)$ such that the forward-kinematic output $\mathbf{G}(\phi_1,\phi_2)$ matches a desired target point $\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}}=[G_x^{\mathrm{des}},G_y^{\mathrm{des}}]^\top$. The implementation follows IK_phi1phi2_from_G in VMC_WN_Cleaned_IK_Function.m.

A numerical solver is used here because the mechanism is not a simple open-chain arm. It is a closed-chain linkage with several dependent internal angles and nested geometric relations in the forward-kinematic model. Because of that, the inverse map from the Cartesian end-point \(\mathbf{G}\) back to \((\phi_1,\phi_2)\) is not convenient to write as one clean closed-form expression over the full usable range.

For that reason, the IK is implemented as a bounded nonlinear least-squares solve. The procedure starts from an initial estimate of the joint angles, evaluates the forward kinematics, measures the position error relative to the target point, and then updates the joint variables until the Cartesian error is minimized within the imposed bounds (MathWorks, n.d.-b).

4.1.2.1 State, Target, and Constraints

The optimization variable is the joint-angle vector

\[ \boldsymbol{\phi} = \begin{bmatrix} \phi_1\\ \phi_2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

This shows that the unknowns in the IK problem are the two actuated joint angles only. The internal linkage angles are not solved independently here, because they are reconstructed automatically when the forward kinematics are evaluated.

and the task-space target is

\[ \mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}} = \begin{bmatrix} G_x^{\mathrm{des}}\\ G_y^{\mathrm{des}} \end{bmatrix}. \]

This is the Cartesian target point for the end of the linkage. The purpose of the IK is to find a pair of joint angles that places the forward-kinematic point \( \mathbf{G} \) at this location.

In the current code, the target is set as $\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}}=[41.56,\,-292.4]^\top$ mm, with box constraints \[ \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{lb}}=[0,\;0]^\top,\qquad \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{ub}}=[140,\;140]^\top \text{ (deg)}. \]

These lower and upper bounds define the physically allowed joint range. This is necessary so the solver does not return angle combinations that may satisfy the mathematics but cannot be achieved by the actual mechanism.

4.1.2.2 Problem Formulation

Define the residual vector using the forward map:

\[ \mathbf{e}(\phi_1,\phi_2) = \mathbf{G}(\phi_1,\phi_2)-\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}} = \begin{bmatrix} G_x(\phi_1,\phi_2)-G_x^{\mathrm{des}}\\ G_y(\phi_1,\phi_2)-G_y^{\mathrm{des}} \end{bmatrix}. \]

This residual vector is the main quantity used in the solve. It compares the Cartesian point predicted by the current angle estimate against the desired target point. If both entries become zero, then the IK has been satisfied exactly.

The bounded nonlinear least-squares objective is

\[ \min_{\phi_1,\phi_2} \; J(\phi_1,\phi_2) =\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{e}(\phi_1,\phi_2)\right\|_2^2 \] subject to \[ \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{lb}} \le \begin{bmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\end{bmatrix} \le \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{ub}}. \]

This objective directly minimizes Cartesian end-point mismatch in millimeters.

The squared-error form is used so that both the horizontal and vertical position errors are reduced together. The solver therefore searches for the angle pair that gives the smallest overall position mismatch while remaining within the joint limits.

4.1.2.3 Numerical Solver Used in Code

In MATLAB, the solution is obtained via lsqnonlin (MathWorks, n.d.-b):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}\phi_1^\star\\\phi_2^\star\end{bmatrix} = \operatorname{lsqnonlin} \!\left( \mathbf{e}(\phi_1,\phi_2),\; \boldsymbol{\phi}_0,\; \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{lb}},\; \boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{ub}} \right). \]

This means MATLAB starts from an initial guess \(\boldsymbol{\phi}_0\), repeatedly evaluates the forward-kinematic error, and updates the joint angles until the residual becomes sufficiently small. The bounds are included directly in the solve so that the search stays within the allowed motion range.

The initial guess is first limited to the allowed range:

\[ \boldsymbol{\phi}_0 \leftarrow \min\!\big(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{ub}}, \max(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\mathrm{lb}},\boldsymbol{\phi}_0)\big). \]

This step clips the initial estimate so that it is valid before the optimization starts. It avoids starting the solve from a point that is already outside the permitted joint range.

After optimization, each angle is normalized by

\[ \mathcal{W}(\alpha)=\operatorname{mod}(\alpha+180,360)-180, \qquad \phi_i^\star \leftarrow \mathcal{W}(\phi_i^\star),\; i\in\{1,2\}. \]

The wrapping step keeps the returned angles in one consistent angle convention. This is useful because different numerical angle values can correspond to the same physical orientation.

The reported solution quality is computed from \[ \mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{err}}= \mathbf{G}(\phi_1^\star,\phi_2^\star)-\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}}, \qquad \|\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{err}}\|_2. \]

This is the final error check. After the solver returns \(\phi_1^\star\) and \(\phi_2^\star\), the forward kinematics are evaluated again to verify how close the resulting point is to the target. A smaller error norm indicates a better IK result.

4.1.2.4 Multi-Start Search

A grid of initial guesses $\boldsymbol{\phi}_0^{(m,n)}=[\phi_{1,m},\phi_{2,n}]^\top$ is evaluated, and the minimum terminal error is selected:

\[ (\phi_1^\dagger,\phi_2^\dagger) = \arg\min_{m,n} \left\| \mathbf{G}\!\left(\phi_{1,m}^\star,\phi_{2,n}^\star\right) -\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{des}} \right\|_2. \]

This step is used because the problem is nonlinear, so different starting guesses can converge to different local solutions. By solving the IK from multiple starting pairs and retaining the smallest final error, the implementation becomes more reliable.

In implementation, each axis is sampled with 7 points over the allowed range, giving $7\times7=49$ starting pairs. The candidate with minimum terminal task-space error is retained.

This strategy improves stability against local minima and reduces sensitivity to one poor initial guess.

This is a repeated-initialization approach in which multiple starting pairs are tested and the solution with the smallest terminal Cartesian error is retained. It is particularly useful for closed-chain geometry where one target point may be associated with more than one nearby solution branch.

4.1.2.5 Practical Interpretation

The IK routine enforces physically admissible joint ranges while solving directly in Cartesian space. Because the mechanism is nonlinear, multiple joint configurations may produce similar end-point locations. The bounded least-squares together with the multi-start search provides a practical and stable inverse solution for later control and firmware integration.

Overall, the IK workflow is to define a target point, assign an initial estimate of the joint angles, run the forward kinematics, evaluate the Cartesian error, refine the joint variables, and retain the best valid solution. In this way, the inverse solution is obtained through a structured optimization procedure rather than by direct algebraic inversion.

4.1.3 Jacobian Torque Mapping

This section explains the Jacobian-based kinematic and force transmission framework implemented in VMC_WN_Jacobian_Transformation.m and VMC_WN_Jacobian_Transformation_Animation.m. The model uses two actuated inputs $(\phi_1,\phi_2)$ and a planar output point \[ \mathbf{G}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}. \]

4.1.3.1 Role of the Jacobian

The instantaneous velocity relation is \[ \dot{\mathbf{G}}= \begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}\\\dot{y}\end{bmatrix} = J(\mathbf{q}) \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\phi}_1\\\dot{\phi}_2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{q}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_1\\\phi_2\end{bmatrix}. \] So joint angular velocities map directly to end-point linear velocity. Physically, the Jacobian is the local transmission operator between actuator space and Cartesian space, which is consistent with standard robot differential kinematics treatment (Khatib, 1987).

4.1.3.2 Physical Meaning of the Matrix Columns

For the 2D, 2-DOF system:

\[ J= \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial \phi_1} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial \phi_2}\\ \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \phi_1} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \phi_2} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Column 1 represents the velocity of $\mathbf{G}$ caused by unit speed at joint 1 with joint 2 fixed; column 2 represents the same for joint 2. This is exactly how the code visualizes Jacobian vectors at point $G$.

4.1.3.3 Numerical Jacobian Used in MATLAB

The implementation computes $J$ numerically via central differences in degrees with step $h=10^{-3}$:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \phi_1}\Big|_{\deg} \approx \frac{\mathbf{G}(\phi_1+h,\phi_2)-\mathbf{G}(\phi_1-h,\phi_2)}{2h}, \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \phi_2}\Big|_{\deg} \approx \frac{\mathbf{G}(\phi_1,\phi_2+h)-\mathbf{G}(\phi_1,\phi_2-h)}{2h}. \] \[ J_{\deg}=\left[\frac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \phi_1}\;\;\frac{\partial \mathbf{G}}{\partial \phi_2}\right]_{\deg}, \qquad J_{\mathrm{rad}}=J_{\deg}\left(\frac{180}{\pi}\right). \]

So $J_{\deg}$ is in mm/deg and $J_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is in mm/rad.

4.1.3.4 Velocity and Acceleration Mapping

Velocity kinematics:

\[ \dot{\mathbf{G}}=J_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}. \]

The code verifies this numerically by finite-difference velocity:

\[ \dot{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{FD}} \approx \frac{\mathbf{G}(t+\Delta t)-\mathbf{G}(t)}{\Delta t}. \]

Acceleration kinematics:

\[ \ddot{\mathbf{G}} = J_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbf{q})\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \dot{J}_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}. \]

A corresponding finite-difference estimate is

\[ \ddot{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{FD}} \approx \frac{\mathbf{G}(t+\Delta t)-2\mathbf{G}(t)+\mathbf{G}(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t^2}. \]

4.1.3.5 Jacobian Transpose Torque Mapping

For end-point force $\mathbf{F}=[F_x,F_y]^\top$ (same global frame as FK), the required joint torques are:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J_{\mathrm{rad}}^\top(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{F}. \]

This follows from power/virtual-work consistency:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau}^\top\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{F}^\top\dot{\mathbf{G}} = \mathbf{F}^\top J\dot{\mathbf{q}} \;\Rightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\tau}=J^\top\mathbf{F}. \]

This Jacobian-transpose force mapping is the same core principle used in operational-space force control (Khatib, 1987).

In the current script, torque is obtained in N$\cdot$mm and converted by: \[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{N\cdot m}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{N\cdot mm}}}{1000}. \]

4.1.3.6 Interpretation of Force-Direction Sweep in Code

The script sweeps unit-force direction $\theta_F\in[-180^\circ,180^\circ]$:

\[ F_x=\cos\theta_F,\qquad F_y=\sin\theta_F,\qquad \|\mathbf{F}\|_2=1, \] \[ \boldsymbol{\tau}(\theta_F)=J^\top\mathbf{F}(\theta_F). \]

It then plots $\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|$, $|\tau_1|$, and $|\tau_2|$ versus force direction. Peaks indicate poor mechanical leverage directions requiring large motor torque.

4.1.3.7 Singularity and Manipulability

The velocity ellipse is generated by \[ \dot{\mathbf{G}}=J_{\mathrm{rad}}\dot{\mathbf{q}},\qquad \|\dot{\mathbf{q}}\|_2=1. \] A scalar indicator used in the script is \[ w=\left|\det\!\left(J_{\mathrm{rad}}\right)\right|. \] Small $w$ corresponds to near-singular behavior: motion capability collapses in one direction and torque demand spikes for certain Cartesian force directions.

4.1.3.8 Control Relevance

In virtual-model or force-based control, a desired Cartesian force $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{des}}$ is first generated from task objectives (e.g., support, compliance, or disturbance rejection), then mapped to actuator commands by \[ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{des}}=J^\top\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{des}}. \] Thus, Jacobian transpose mapping is the key bridge between endpoint force objectives and motor torque actuation.
